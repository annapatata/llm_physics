\begin{abstract}
We present a partial reproduction of \citet{allen-zhu2023physics} (\emph{Physics of Language Models: Part 1, Context-Free Grammar}), which studies how transformer language models learn the latent hierarchical structure of context-free grammars (CFGs). We re-implement the training and evaluation pipelines, train a GPT-2 model with rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) on the \texttt{cfg3b} synthetic grammar for 6{,}500 steps, and evaluate it through completion accuracy, KL divergence, linear probing of hidden states, and attention-pattern analysis. Our results largely confirm the qualitative findings of the original paper, while quantitative gaps—particularly in KL divergence—are attributable to the reduced training budget.
\end{abstract}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A central question in the study of large language models is whether next-token prediction alone is sufficient for a model to discover the latent structure of the language it is trained on. \citet{allen-zhu2023physics} investigate this in a controlled setting: synthetic languages generated by context-free grammars (CFGs), where ground-truth parse trees are available by construction. This allows precise, quantitative questions to be asked about the model's internal representations.

We reproduce their experimental setup using a standard GPT-2 architecture. Among the synthetic grammars introduced by \citet{allen-zhu2023physics}, we focus on \texttt{cfg3b}, the easiest of the family: under computational constraints, its lower complexity makes it the most tractable grammar to learn within a limited training budget, while still exhibiting the deep hierarchical structure central to the paper's analysis. Our goal is to verify whether the original conclusions hold under a reduced training budget (6{,}500 vs.\ the paper's 100{,}000 iterations).

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Context-Free Grammars as Synthetic Languages}

A context-free grammar $G = (V_N, V_T, R, S)$ defines a language over terminal symbols $V_T$ via rewriting rules in $R$. Each string $x = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ is derived from the start symbol $S$ through $L$ levels of non-terminal expansions. At each level $\ell$, every terminal position $i$ has a unique ancestor non-terminal symbol $s_\ell(i)$ and ancestor index $p_\ell(i)$ (the ordinal position of that non-terminal among all level-$\ell$ nodes in the parse tree, left to right). A \emph{boundary} at level $\ell$ and position $i$ is defined as:
\[
b_\ell(i) = \mathbf{1}[p_\ell(i) \neq p_\ell(i+1)],
\]
i.e., position $i$ is the last terminal of a constituent at level $\ell$.

The grammar \texttt{cfg3b} contains 32 production rules over 16 non-terminal symbols and 3 terminal symbols, with a hierarchy exactly 7 levels deep and branching factors of 2 or 3. Its level structure is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cfg3b}. The exact rules are excluded for readability but can be found in the original \citet{allen-zhu2023physics}.

\subsection{Probing Hidden States}

Following \citet{allen-zhu2023physics}, we use \emph{multi-head linear probing} to test whether the hidden state $h_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ at position $i$ encodes structural information. A probe $f_\theta(h_i)$ is trained to predict the ancestor symbol $s_\ell(i)$.

Two probe variants are used:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0pt]
  \item[Full probe (R4).] Unrestricted attention; accuracy evaluated over all positions.
  \item[Diagonal probe (R5).] Attention restricted to a band of width $2\delta+1$ around the diagonal; accuracy evaluated at boundary positions only.
\end{description}

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Model and Training}

We train a GPT-2 model \citep{radford2019gpt2} with rotary positional embeddings (RoPE, \texttt{gpt\_rot}): 12 layers, 12 heads, embedding dimension $d = 768$, sequence length 512, vocabulary size 5 ($<BOS>, 1, 2, 3, <EOS>$).

Training uses AdamW ($\beta_1{=}0.9$, $\beta_2{=}0.98$, weight decay $0.1$, learning rate $3{\times}10^{-4}$) with a linear decay schedule over $N{=}6{,}500$ steps. We use gradient accumulation over 8 micro-batches of size 12 (effective batch size 96). Strings are sampled on-the-fly from \texttt{cfg3b}.

\subsection{Evaluation}

We evaluate the model on four tasks:

\textbf{Completion accuracy (R1).} The model generates a completion autoregressively from a prefix of length 0 or 50. A completion is correct if the full string is a valid string of the grammar. Reported under both multinomial sampling and greedy decoding.

\textbf{KL divergence (R3).} We compute $\text{KL}(P_{\text{true}} \| P_{\text{model}})$ averaged over positions across 200 sampled strings.

\textbf{Probing (R4--R5).} For each non-terminal level $\ell$, we train a multi-head linear probe for 5{,}000 iterations on the frozen hidden states of the last transformer block.

\textbf{Attention analysis (R6--R9).} We measure how attention is distributed relative to token distance and constituent boundaries.

At non-boundary positions, the hidden state is consistent with multiple possible subtrees, making classification information-theoretically impossible—a result formalised in the original paper.

\paragraph{Result 6 — Distance-based attention}
Averaging attention weights $\bar{A}_p$ over all pairs $(i,j)$ with $j-i=p$, across all layers and heads, we observe a strong recency bias. This distance bias is subtracted to isolate structure-sensitive attention.

\section{Extension: Present vs.\ Used Information}
\label{sec:extension}

\subsection{Motivation and Target Level}

The probing experiments (R4--R5) establish that NT ancestry information is \emph{present} in the model's hidden states. Our extension asks a sharper question: is this information also \emph{used} by the model when generating subsequent tokens? We investigate this via activation patching—surgically replacing the hidden state at specific positions and measuring the effect on the model's output.

We focus on level NT5 as the target because it occupies a structural sweet spot in the hierarchy. An NT5 constituent spans several terminals (roughly $4$--$9$): deep enough to be a coherent sub-structure, yet high enough that corrupting it breaks the \emph{global} parse structure (CYK validity) while leaving the \emph{local} surface statistics (n-gram texture) largely intact. This structure-vs-surface dissociation is exactly what separates ``the information is used'' from ``we perturbed the model and it got worse.''

Both higher and lower levels lose this dissociation. A higher level such as NT2 dominates almost the entire string, so corrupting it collapses the output wholesale, leaving little local structure to preserve. NT6, one level above the terminals, is the opposite failure: an NT6 constituent expands to only $2$--$3$ terminals, so NT6 essentially \emph{is} the local bigram structure. Its boundaries are also dense (${\approx}40\%$ of positions), so the intervention---which we confine to a fixed-length prefix (Section~\ref{sec:patch-exp})---would overwrite many positions for NT6 but only a handful for NT5. Patching NT6 is therefore a blunt, high-volume overwrite that moves the surface metric together with the structural one, so the two can no longer be dissociated. NT5 is the level at which a coherent structural unit can be swapped without dragging the local surface along with it.

\subsection{Activation Patching Experiment}
\label{sec:patch-exp}

Each trial starts from a \emph{clean} CFG string $x$. We form the prefix $[\mathrm{BOS}] + x_{1:50}$ (the first $50$ terminals) and let the model generate a continuation autoregressively. The intervention is confined to this $50$-token prefix: on every forward pass a hook at layer 5 overwrites the residual stream at the prefix's NT5 boundary positions before the remaining blocks run. The generated continuation tokens are never patched directly---the corruption can reach them only by attending back onto the patched prefix positions---and we then score the completion with CYK. We compare three sources for the overwriting state against the unpatched clean baseline:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0pt]
  \item[Donor.] Hidden states copied from a different valid string (the \emph{donor}) at the same prefix positions, chosen so the swap replaces the NT5 identity (criteria below). This tests whether swapping the NT5 label at the boundary causally alters the output structure.
  \item[Noise (boundary).] Gaussian noise (zero-mean, variance matched to the residual stream) injected at the NT5 boundary positions only.
  \item[Noise (non-boundary).] Gaussian noise injected at the same \emph{number} of non-boundary positions, chosen at random. This is the control: if structure is stored at boundaries, corrupting non-boundaries should have little effect.
\end{description}

A donor string is accepted only if it (i) has a level-5 boundary at the \emph{same absolute prefix index} as the clean string for at least three positions, and (ii) carries a \emph{different} valid NT5 identity at each of those shared positions; we patch exactly those shared positions. The same-index requirement matters because the model uses RoPE: a hidden state encodes relative position, so injecting a donor's state from a different index would add a positional confound (it also forces the donor to be at least as long as the patched index). The different-identity requirement ensures the swap actually changes the parse---copying the same NT5 would be a near no-op. Donors are otherwise drawn from the natural CFG length distribution rather than length-matched, so that the higher-level information that unavoidably rides along (below) varies randomly across trials instead of systematically.

Patching an NT5 boundary does not swap the NT5 identity in isolation: there is one hidden vector per position, with no separate slot per level, so the donor's vector also carries its higher-level ancestors (NT2--NT4) and its NT6 child. The two contaminants behave differently. Higher-level information varies randomly across the 500 randomly drawn donors, so it averages out and the systematic NT5 swap dominates. The NT6 identity, however, cannot be averaged away: a level-5 boundary is necessarily a level-6 boundary as well, so an NT6 child is swapped on \emph{every} trial. We accept this as a limitation---cleanly isolating NT5 from NT6 would require overwriting only the probe's NT5 subspace direction rather than the full vector, which we leave to future work.

\subsection{Discussion}

The donor condition produces a 57.6\% drop in CYK validity, confirming that the NT5 identity stored at boundary positions is causally \emph{used} during generation: replacing it with a structurally incompatible---but individually valid---NT5 label drives the model to complete the string ungrammatically. The noise-at-boundary condition causes a smaller but still substantial drop (29.0\%), confirming that these positions carry load-bearing information; the larger donor effect indicates the collapse is driven by the \emph{identity} swap, not merely by perturbing those positions. Because the donor supplies a coherent, valid alternative parse rather than random corruption, the model reads a plausible-looking signal and follows it confidently---it is, in effect, ``tricked'' into committing to a competing structure that conflicts with the unpatched context---which is precisely why the donor breaks CYK more sharply than noise does.

Noise injected at \emph{non-boundary} positions causes almost no structural disruption (4.8\% drop, against a 98.8\% clean baseline). This asymmetry---a 29.0\% drop when the same noise hits boundaries versus 4.8\% when it hits non-boundaries---is strong causal evidence for the original paper's hypothesis: structural information is not spread uniformly across positions but concentrated at constituent boundaries, from which downstream layers read it to guide generation.

The $n$-gram KL columns test whether these interventions break \emph{structure} specifically or simply degrade the output. Under the donor patch, the model's bigram texture stays at the clean baseline ($0.0001$ vs.\ $0.0000$) even as CYK validity collapses: the model keeps emitting locally well-formed token transitions while failing to close the global parse. This structure-vs-surface dissociation is what makes the result a \emph{use} test rather than ``we perturbed the model and it got worse.'' We caution, however, that the surface axis is weakly resolved here: all $n$-gram KL values lie within $0.002$ of zero, so there is little dynamic range on these short continuations. We had expected the donor's KL to climb with $n$-gram order (local texture intact, longer-range structure breaking); instead it stays flat across orders, and the only appreciable movement is in the non-boundary noise condition at orders 3--4 ($0.0013$ and $0.0019$). We do not over-interpret this small, prediction-contrary drift; cleanly resolving the surface axis would require longer continuations and a larger sample.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our results confirm the central qualitative findings of \citet{allen-zhu2023physics}: after training on next-token prediction over CFG-generated strings, a GPT-2 model does develop internal representations that are sensitive to parse-tree structure. The attention analysis (R6--R9) is particularly striking—the DP-like attention pattern at constituent boundaries emerges clearly even at 6{,}500 training steps.

The high multinomial completion accuracy (98.00\%) is consistent with the relatively small KL divergence (0.000350 nats/token): the model has already learned to place most of its probability mass over valid continuations. The remaining KL gap relative to the paper's target (${\approx}0.00009$) reflects that the model has not yet fully converged to the exact grammar distribution, but the bulk of the structural learning has already occurred.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

We note first that further training is not a priority for this project. Our goal is to \emph{reproduce} the qualitative findings of \citet{allen-zhu2023physics} rather than to ship a converged model, and the structural phenomena we set out to verify---the DP-like boundary attention, the decodability of parse-tree symbols, and the causal use of NT5 boundaries---are already clearly present at 6{,}500 steps. Closing the residual KL gap to the paper's target would mostly confirm convergence we already expect; it would not change any of the conclusions above. We therefore see the more interesting directions in the extension itself, where two issues are worth pursuing.

\paragraph{Cleaner isolation of NT5.} Our patch overwrites the full residual vector at a boundary, which unavoidably swaps the NT5 child's NT6 constituent on every trial (Section~\ref{sec:patch-exp}). Averaging over random donors removes the higher-level contamination but not this nested NT6 swap, so the donor effect mixes ``NT5 was changed'' with ``its NT6 child was changed.'' A cleaner intervention would overwrite only the probe's NT5 subspace direction---projecting the donor's contribution onto the NT5 readout and leaving the orthogonal complement (including the NT6 signal) untouched---so the swap targets NT5 alone.

\paragraph{Better-resolved local-structure measurement.} The $n$-gram KL columns were meant to separate a broken \emph{global} parse from intact \emph{local} surface texture, but the surface axis is weakly resolved on our short continuations: all values sit within $0.002$ of zero and the donor's KL does not grow with $n$-gram order as predicted (Table~\ref{tab:patching}). A sharper version of this experiment would use longer continuations and a larger trial count to give the surface metric real dynamic range, and could compare against the grammar's true $n$-gram distribution at several orders to trace exactly where, in $n$, the corruption transitions from local to structural.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have reproduced the experimental setup of \citet{allen-zhu2023physics} on the \texttt{cfg3b} grammar using a GPT-2 model trained for 6{,}500 steps. Our results confirm the paper's qualitative findings—particularly the DP-like attention pattern at constituent boundaries and the sensitivity of linear probes to parse-tree structure—while a remaining gap in KL divergence reflects incomplete convergence at our reduced training budget. The codebase is available at \url{https://github.com/annapatata/llm_physics}.
